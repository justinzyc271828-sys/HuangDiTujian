# The Later Deposed Emperor · Principal Deeds

Liu Yu, eldest son of Emperor Ming, took the throne in the 1st year of Taiyu at ten, with Chu Yuan, Yuan Can, Cai Xingzong, Shen Youzhi, and Xiao Daocheng governing by the late will. The Later Deposed Emperor was "lawless and fond of killing": the records tell of his secret jaunts by night, "walking or riding into the wilds with a few attendants," and his cruelties against the people — "clubbing heads, gouging hearts" — until court and realm feared him as a beast.

In the 2nd year of Yuanhui the Prince of Guiyang, Xiufan, rose and struck at Jiankang; Xiao Daocheng and the others put it down, and Daocheng's prestige rose daily. In the 5th year of Yuanhui, the emperor moved to kill Daocheng; Daocheng struck first, and his man Yang Yufu slew the emperor in the Renshou Hall, aged fifteen. The Prince of Ancheng, Zhun, was set up — Emperor Shun — and two years later Daocheng received the abdication: Liu Song was no more.

Five years on the throne, and the pattern of "a child sovereign, a doubtful state, strong ministers grown great" was complete: a child playing and killing, ministers deposing and enthroning at will. Daocheng walked the standard road of the southern usurper — merit in pacification, a slain sovereign, an abdication. The histories see the Later Deposed Emperor as no less a victim of the southern system than a monster: the fall of Liu Song was not the work of one child but of the accumulated disease of its princely politics.

> References: the punctuated critical edition of the *Songshu*, ch. 9 ("Benji of the Later Deposed Emperor"), and the *Nanqishu*, ch. 1 ("Benji of Emperor Gao").
