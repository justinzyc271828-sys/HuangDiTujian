# Li Yuanhao · Key Events

Li Yuanhao was the de facto founder of Western Xia. In 1032 his father Li Deming died and he succeeded as Prince of Xia, at once accelerating de-Songification and nation-building; in 1038 he proclaimed himself emperor at Xingqing, took the dynastic name Great Xia, and changed the era to Tianshou Lifa Yanzuo, formally standing alongside Song and Liao. This page takes 《宋史·卷485·夏国传》 as the backbone of its chronology, with the relevant annals of 《辽史》 as a secondary entry point.

Around his proclamation he completed a whole set of national symbols and institutions: he ordered Yeli Renrong and others to devise the Tangut script, reformed dress and officialdom, and established organs such as the Secretariat and the Bureau of Military Affairs under separate Tangut and Han designations. This line of "asserting distinctiveness" ran in exactly the opposite direction from the sinicization of [[n-wei-xiaowen|Emperor Xiaowen of Northern Wei]]; read together, they show the two extreme paths by which medieval northern regimes handled their own identity.

On the military side, around 1036 he seized Gua, Sha, Su, and other key points of the Hexi Corridor, laying the territorial foundation for his imperial claim—the very corridor opened in the age of [[han-wu-di|Emperor Wu of Han]] as the northwest lifeline, which a thousand years later became the ground on which Great Xia was founded. At Sanchuankou in 1040, Haoshuichuan in 1041, and Dingchuanzhai in 1042, Xia forces mauled the Song armies three years running and established Western Xia's military standing; Haoshuichuan in particular is the famous set piece of the Song–Xia war (cross-referenced in the biographies of Ren Fu and others in 《宋史》).

After the string of victories came the diplomatic wrap-up. In 1044 Song and Xia reached the Qingli settlement: Yuanhao styled himself a subject toward Song while reigning as emperor within his own realm, trading the split between name and fact for annual payments and a stable border; the same year, after clashes with Liao, he made peace, maneuvering deftly within the Song–Liao–Xia triangle. Civil construction, military success, and statecraft together had by now raised a Hexi regime into one pole of a tripartite standoff.

In 1048 Yuanhao was stabbed to death amid palace struggles; the child ruler Liangzuo succeeded, and the consort clan Mozang took power—the dynasty did not end with his person, and Western Xia passed to its later rulers. It should be noted that Song and Liao historiography is biased against Western Xia, so this project's appraisal takes a middle position; details such as the creation of the Tangut script still await confirmation from specialized scholarship.
