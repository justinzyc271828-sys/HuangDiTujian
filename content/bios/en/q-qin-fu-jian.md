# Shizu of Former Qin · Principal Deeds

Fu Jian (r. 357–385) was the Former Qin ruler who came closest to unifying all under Heaven in the Sixteen Kingdoms period. In Yongxing 1 (357) he executed the tyrannical ruler Fu Sheng, took the title Heavenly King, and changed the era name to Yongxing; Former Qin thereby entered the age of Fu Jian. This page takes the 《晋书·苻坚载记》 (juan 113–114) as its chronological backbone, checked against the chronicle of the 《资治通鉴·晋纪》.

After his enthronement he entrusted the affairs of state to Wang Meng. Wang Meng disciplined the bureaucracy and struck at powerful kinsmen; Former Qin's strength surged, forming the civil-government foundation of Fu Jian's reign. Military achievement followed: in Taihe 5 (370) Wang Meng took Ye, Former Yan was destroyed, and the unification of the north accelerated; in Taiyuan 1 (376) he further destroyed Former Liang, reaching as far as the land of Dai; Hexi fell to Former Qin, the north was largely unified, and the map reached its peak.

After the peak came the sharp turn. In Taiyuan 7 (382) the court debated attacking Jin; Wang Meng was already dead, and remonstrance carried no force. The next year Fu Jian resolved on the southern campaign — the histories record boasts of the sort "throw our whips into the river and they would dam its flow" — and mobilized the entire state. In Taiyuan 8 (383), at the single battle of the Fei River, he was crushingly defeated by the Eastern Jin line of Xie An and Xie Xuan; Former Qin disintegrated and the various peoples rose again. It should be noted that the phrase "whips damming the river" carries a strong literary coloring in the sources, but strategic responsibility for the southern campaign rests with Fu Jian himself — that point still stands.

In Taiyuan 9 (384) Murong Chui declared independence and the Yan-Qin order collapsed; the next year Fu Jian fled west to the Wujiangshan area, where he was killed by Yao Chang, and Former Qin rapidly fell apart. Reading Fu Jian, one may set him against [[n-wei-taiwu|Emperor Taiwu of Northern Wei]]: both integrated the north by force; one broke on a southern campaign and lost his state, the other re-unified the north and opened the Northern Dynasties — together they show the rhythm of rise and fall across the Sixteen Kingdoms and Northern Dynasties.
