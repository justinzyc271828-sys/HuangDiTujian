# The Last Sovereign of Wu · Principal Deeds

Sun Hao, styled Yuanzong, was a grandson of Sun Quan, son of the deposed crown prince He, enfeoffed Marquis of Wucheng. In the 6th year of Yong'an, after Sun Xiu's death, the chancellor Puru Yangxing and General Zhang Bu, deeming the child heir too weak, installed Hao, changing the era to Yuanxing. At first he issued kind decrees, relieved the people, and opened the granaries, winning a fine reputation throughout the court; within months his savagery showed itself — in the 1st year of Yuanxing he slew Puru Yangxing and Zhang Bu, the very men who had enthroned him, and the age of reckless killing opened.

His tyranny fills the histories: a lover of wine who forced his ministers drunk to catch their slips; a killer who used flaying and eye-gouging among his torments; he favored the flatterer He Ding, slew the attendant Zhang Shang, and closed the road of remonstrance. In the 1st year of Ganlu he moved the capital to Wuchang until the people's cry — "rather drink Jianye's water than eat Wuchang's fish; rather die back in Jianye than dwell in Wuchang" — forced his return. Era followed era to match portents — Tiance, Tianxi — and the government fell into chaos.

In the 4th year of Tianji Emperor Wu of Jin invaded on six roads: Wang Jun's fleet came down from Yizhou, Du Yu and Wang Hun pressed from every side; Lu Kang was dead, and Wu had no great general left. In the third month Wang Jun reached Jianye, and Hao, bound bare with the coffin in his cart, surrendered at the army gate — Wu, fifty-two years from Sun Quan's imperial title, was no more, and the realm was reunified under Jin.

Brought with his house to Luoyang, he was made Marquis of Guiming by Jin, and died there in the 4th year of Taikang at forty-two. The historians' verdict: "brutal, stubborn, arrogant, cruel beyond measure" — Wu fell not to the strength of Jin but to his own tyranny: "he had lost the people's hearts and Heaven's mandate alike." No fallen sovereign surpasses him in infamy. Yet his opening reforms show a man of some ability; the tyrant in him, once loosed, could not be checked — a lesson for unchecked power.
