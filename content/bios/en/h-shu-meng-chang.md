# Meng Chang, The Last Sovereign of Later Shu · Principal Deeds

Meng Chang, third son of Meng Zhixiang, took the throne in the 1st year of Mingde (934) at sixteen. Thirty-one years he reigned — among the longest of the Ten Kingdoms: Shu "at peace," and Chang, a lover of letters, feasting and writing verses with his Consort Huarui; he had the stone classics of the *Analects* and the *Erya* cut in the Chengdu academy — the culture of Later Shu "not behind the Central Plains."

But "long peace bred ease and pleasure": in his last years he "grew luxurious and idle," trusting the incapable Wang Zhaoyuan, "the army and the government unrepaired." In the 3rd year of Qiande (965) Emperor Taizu of Song sent Wang Quanbin to invade; the Song army came through the Jianmen Pass, Meng Chang surrendered, and Later Shu was no more — thirty-two years from Zhixiang's title, two rulers. Brought to Kaifeng, he was made Duke of Qin and died that same year.

The histories say: "he lost the realm to ease and pleasure." Later Shu held the shield of the two Chuan and the wealth of the heavenly prefecture, "the spring of the Jinjiang"; but "born in ease, dead in ease" — Chang fell not to Song's strength but to the two words "ease and pleasure." His fall began the closing of the Ten: Southern Han, Southern Tang, and Wuyue followed into Song.

> References: the punctuated critical edition of the *Xin Wudaishi*, ch. 64 ("Annals of Later Shu"), and the *Songshi*, ch. 479 (the Meng of Xishu).
