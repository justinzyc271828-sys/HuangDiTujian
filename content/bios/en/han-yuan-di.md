# Emperor Yuan of Han · Principal Deeds

Liu Shi was the son of [[han-xuan-di|Emperor Xuan of Han]]. Even when Shi was made heir apparent, Xuan had misgivings: the heir was gentle and devoted to Confucian learning, and Xuan once sighed that "the one who will throw my house into disorder is the heir apparent." When Xuan died in the first Huanglong year, Shi succeeded, and Western Han turned from the pragmatic "mixed way of king and hegemon" to Confucian politics — the hinge of the dynasty's decline.

His reign opened with some promise: Confucian scholars such as Gong Yu were summoned, and the garrisons of the Ganquan and Jianzhang palaces were debated and trimmed. But the edicts dissolved into formalism, and irresolution became Yuan's chronic ailment; Shi Xian, chief of the Inner Secretariat, seized power and clogged the court. On the frontier, Feng Fengshi crushed a Qiang revolt in Longxi in the second Yongguang year, and in the third Jianzhao year Gan Yanshou and Chen Tang, acting on their own authority, slew the Zhizhi chanyu in Kangju — the boast "whoever offends mighty Han shall be punished, however distant" dates from that campaign — yet all was the work of ministers, with the emperor having no part.

In the first Jingning year the Huhanye chanyu came to court seeking a bride, and Yuan granted him the palace woman Wang Zhaojun; her departure became the last great chapter of Han-Xiongnu marriage alliance. In the fifth month of the same year, Yuan died and [[han-cheng-di|Emperor Cheng]] succeeded. The historians wrote that Yuan was "fettered by the letter of the classics, irresolute to the end"; in the Yuan-Cheng age, consort-clan and eunuch problems accumulated in turn, and the decline of the house of Han began here.
