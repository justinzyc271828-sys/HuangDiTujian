# Emperor Cheng of Han · Principal Deeds

Liu Ao, heir of [[han-yuan-di|Emperor Yuan of Han]], was born in the Jiaguan painted hall and came to the throne with every expectation of the empire's crown prince. Yet the politics of his reign became the classic specimen of Western Han's consort-clan disease: in the first year after accession he enfeoffed his maternal uncles, and the Wang brothers and nephews filled the court — Wang Feng, Wang Shang, Wang Yin, and Wang Gen governing in turn, with "five Wang marquises in a single day" as the byword of the age.

Cheng himself was sunk in wine and women, leaving government to his mother's family: in the first Heping year the Yellow River broke at Dongjun, making river control a great matter of state; in the Hongjia-Yongshi years the Changling mausoleum project consumed enormous resources before being abandoned in favor of Yanling; and in the harem the crises ran deeper — Empress Xu was deposed in the third Hongjia year, and in the first Yongshi year Zhao Feiyan was made empress, the Zhao sisters monopolizing favor for a decade while the emperor produced no heir. In the first Suihe year he made Liu Xin, king of Dingtao, heir apparent — the future [[han-ai-di|Emperor Ai]].

In the second Suihe year, Wang Gen recommended his nephew Wang Mang as Grand Commander-in-Chief, carrying Wang power to its peak; in the third month Cheng died suddenly at forty-five. The historians' verdict: "sunk in wine and women; the Zhaos threw the inner court into disorder; the maternal kin ran the government." In twenty-six years the tumor in the structure of the Han state grew complete — consort power, harem intrigue, and a throne without an heir — and [[xin-wang-mang|Wang Mang]] grew from exactly that soil. The fall of Western Han was largely seeded in the reign of Cheng.

> References: the punctuated critical edition of the *Hanshu*, ch. 10 (Benji of Emperor Cheng), the biography of Empress Yuan, and the biography of the Consorts; Tan Qixiang's atlas, vol. 2.
