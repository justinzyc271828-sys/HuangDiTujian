# Emperor Ming of Han · Principal Deeds

Liu Zhuang was the fourth son of [[e-han-guangwu|Emperor Guangwu of Han]], by Empress Yin. In the nineteenth Jianwu year, with Empress Guo deposed and Yin raised in her place, Zhuang became heir apparent, studying the *Documents* under Huan Rong. When Guangwu died in the second Zhongyuan year, Zhuang took the throne at thirty — inheriting an empire just reunited, to which he gave the keynote of strict discipline.

He governed by law and regulation: no family of the inner palace might be enfeoffed or take part in government; when Princess Guantao asked an office for her son, Ming refused and gave her ten million cash instead. The case of Prince Ying of Chu drew a wide net — harsh, perhaps, but proof that he would not spare the favored. In the Yongping years the Northern Suburb rites, caps, and carriage regalia were settled one by one; in mid-Yongping the Twenty-eight Generals were painted in the Yuntai Hall in memory of the founders; and in the eighth Yongping year an edict answered Prince Ying's patronage of "upasaka and shramana" feasts — among the earliest mentions of Buddhism in official documents, a first trace of the Dharma's entry into China. In the twelfth Yongping year Wang Jing was sent to repair the Bian canal from Xingyang to the Qiansheng estuary, separating river and canal — the greatest water project of Eastern Han.

In the sixteenth Yongping year Dou Gu struck the northern Xiongnu and took the Yiwulu lands, installing the Yihe commandant; in the same year Ban Chao and Guo Xun were sent to the Western Regions, and the next year Cheshi was taken and the states of the Western Regions again sent sons to serve at court — the Eastern Han's opening of the west was settled in Ming's reign. In the eighth month of the eighteenth Yongping year he died in the front hall of the Eastern Palace at forty-eight, his will ordering no memorial temple. What he left [[e-han-zhang|Emperor Zhang]] was a court where law was clear and affairs in order; the paired fame of the Ming-Zhang age rests on exactly this.

> References: the punctuated critical edition of the *Hou Hanshu*, ch. 2 (Benji of Emperor Ming), and the biographies of Ban Chao, Wang Jing, and Prince Ying of Chu; Tan Qixiang's atlas, vol. 2.
