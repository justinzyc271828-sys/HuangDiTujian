# Gaozu of Later Zhao · Principal Deeds

Shi Le was a Jie. In his youth, around the Yongxing era of the Western Jin, he was kidnapped and sold into slavery; he later gathered a following, rose in arms, and finally ascended the imperial throne — the span from slave to Son of Heaven makes him the most dramatic state-founder in the history of the Sixteen Kingdoms. This page takes the Shi Le annals in juan 104–105 of the 《晋书》 as its chronological backbone, supplemented by the chronicle of the 《资治通鉴》 (available in the local reference library).

At the start of his enterprise he attached himself for a time to the Han-Zhao system of Liu Yuan and Liu Cong, building strength through campaigns in north China under the Han-Zhao banner. Around the fall of Luoyang in Yongjia 5 (311), he joined the melee that followed the Yongjia upheaval in north China; his military power swelled rapidly, making him a leading force among the regional powers.

In Taixing 2 (319) Shi Le took the title King of Zhao and made Xiangguo his capital, formally stepping out of Former Zhao's shadow — the Later Zhao regime was thereby founded; two years later he advanced to Great Chanyu, his titles and institutions rising in step. In Xianhe 3–4 (328–329) he fought the decisive battle along the Luoyang line against Liu Yao, the last ruler of Former Zhao: he defeated Liu Yao, destroyed Former Zhao, and most of the north entered the Later Zhao map. In Xianhe 5 (330) he proclaimed himself emperor at Xiangguo, completing the imperial system — and with it the entire passage from slave to Son of Heaven.

Shi Le was not a man of martial feats alone. He made Zhang Bin his chief strategist, promoted schools and fixed institutions, established the Gentlemen's Camp to house men of letters, and explored the building of order within the framework of Hu-Han dual rule. One caveat: the records of Later Zhao cruelty in the histories must often be divided between Shi Le himself and his successor Shi Hu; of Shi Le himself, historians still speak of his boldness of design.

In Xianhe 8 (333) Shi Le died at Xiangguo. Shi Hong, the heir he had arranged, was at once dispossessed by Shi Hu; Later Zhao politics deteriorated quickly, and the dynastic line did not last long. Read him against [[q-qin-fu-jian|Fu Jian of Former Qin]]: both were founders or restorers of Hu states in the Sixteen Kingdoms period, one earlier and one later, and together they show the era's different paths of state-building and of order and collapse.
