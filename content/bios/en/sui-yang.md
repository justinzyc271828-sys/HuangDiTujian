# Emperor Yang of Sui · Major Deeds

Yang Guang was originally the Prince of Jin. In Kaihuang 9 (589) he took part in the pacification of Chen and earned merit; in Kaihuang 20 (600) he was made crown prince; in Renshou 4 (604) he ascended the throne, later changing the era name to Daye. This page follows the Annals of Emperor Yang in the Book of Sui as its chronological spine, cross-checked against the Sui chapters of the Zizhi Tongjian.

During the Daye era he launched one outsized project and operation after another: in Daye 1 (605) he built the Eastern Capital at Luoyang and dug the Tongji Canal and other canal sections, linking north-south water transport; in Daye 4 (608) he dug the Yongji Canal, reaching Zhuo Commandery in the north; in Daye 5 (609) he toured west to Zhangye, where Hu merchants from the Western Regions came to court, presenting for a time the appearance of a flourishing age. The Eastern Capital and the canals form his most important lasting legacy, yet the sheer scale of conscription was simultaneously consuming the empire's foundations.

The turning point came in Daye 8 (612) with the first campaign against Goguryeo: a massive eastern expedition that lost armies and drained wealth, intensifying domestic tensions. The following year (613) he campaigned in Liaodong again, and Yang Xuangan rose in revolt, accelerating the collapse. In Daye 12 (616) he went to Jiangdu for the third time, and the Central Plains slipped from control; in Daye 13 (617) warlords rose on all sides and the central authority existed in name only; in Daye 14 (618), Yuwen Huaji and others launched the Jiangdu coup, Emperor Yang was killed, and the Sui fell.

Later generations judged him extremely harshly, yet the lasting effects of the canals, the Eastern Capital, and the linking of north and south run deep. Read alongside [[sui-wen|Emperor Wen of Sui]], one can see how the accumulation of the Kaihuang era was pushed to the extreme and exhausted during the Daye years; read alongside [[tang-tai-zong|Emperor Taizong of Tang]], one can see how the Tang both inherited and reacted against Sui institutions.
