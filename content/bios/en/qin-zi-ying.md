# Ziying, King of Qin · Principal Deeds

Ziying's parentage is disputed in the histories — some call him the Second Emperor's nephew, some the First Emperor's younger brother, some Fu Su's son; the *Shiji*'s "Basic Annals of Qin" records that he was "benevolent and frugal, and the people all praised his words." In the third year of the Second Emperor, Zhao Gao slew the Second Emperor at Wangyi Palace and, failing to take the throne himself against the ministers' will, set up Ziying, dropping the imperial title for that of king. Ziying knew his malice: on the fifth day of his reign, plotting with his two sons and the eunuch Han Tan, he stabbed Zhao Gao down in the fasting palace and exterminated his kin in Xianyang.

With Gao gone, Qin was past saving. Liu Bang broke the Wu Pass, took Lantian, and came to Bashang; Ziying, a silken cord about his neck, riding in a white-draped cart and bearing the imperial seal, surrendered beside the Zhidao road. The Qin altars, after two emperors, were no more — Ziying had reigned forty-six days. Xiang Yu then entered the pass, sacked Xianyang, burned the palaces, and put Ziying to death, cutting off Qin's ancestral sacrifices forever.

Ziying enters the histories as the last king of Qin. He never bore the imperial title and stands outside the imperial line, so the gazetteer lists him as quasi-emperor. His resolution in slaying Zhao Gao shows a ruler equal to a desperate hour; but inheriting the ruin of the book-burning and scholar-burying age, against the converging six states, even a worthy sovereign could not have turned the tide. The historians' regret is a vindication: Qin fell not by his hand but by the tyranny of the First and Second Emperors.
