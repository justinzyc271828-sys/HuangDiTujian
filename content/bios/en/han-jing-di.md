# Emperor Jing of Han · Principal Deeds

Liu Qi was the son of [[han-wen-di|Emperor Wen of Han]] and succeeded as heir apparent. He continued Wen's policy of rest, easing flogging and lightening punishment, and might have reigned in quiet continuity — until his plan to trim the feudatories touched off the greatest civil war of early Han, the Revolt of the Seven States, which he then suppressed with an iron hand, tipping the balance between throne and feudatories decisively toward Chang'an.

The revolt began with the trimming plan of Censor-in-Chief Chao Cuo: cutting the commanderies of Wu and Chu, the kings of Wu and Chu and five other states rose and marched west under the slogan "execute Chao Cuo." Persuaded by Yuan Ang, Emperor Jing had Chao Cuo beheaded at the East Market to appease the rebels; the armies pressed on regardless, and the court saw that the revolt was never about Chao Cuo. Thereafter Jing leaned on Prince Xiao of Liang holding Suiyang, and entrusted Zhou Yafu, who held fast at Changyi, cut Wu's grain route, and broke Wu and Chu within three months; all seven states were settled. After that war, the enfeoffment order of early Han was changed in substance, and centralization was secured.

The years after the revolt were not tranquil: the heir of the Li clan was set aside in favor of Jiaodong's king [[han-wu-di|Liu Che]]; Rong, the deposed prince of Linjiang, was summoned and died by his own hand; and Zhou Yafu, whose merit now overshadowed the throne, ended in prison, starving himself to death on the fifth day. The historians censured the emperor here — cold and ungrateful, they said, and lacking Wen's generosity — yet in institutional terms, the trimming of the feudatories and the concentration of power were exactly the precondition of Emperor Wu's golden age.

In the first month of the later third year, Emperor Jing died in the Weiyang Palace and Crown Prince Che succeeded. Four decades of Wen-Jing recuperation now passed into the hands of the most expansionist emperor in Chinese history; and "the Wen-Jing recovery," the byword for the House of Han's foundations, became the hinge between [[han-gao-zu|Emperor Gaozu]]'s founding and Emperor Wu's frontier expansion.
