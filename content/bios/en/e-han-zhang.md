# Emperor Zhang of Han · Principal Deeds

Liu Da was the son of [[e-han-ming|Emperor Ming of Han]]. Made heir apparent in the third Yongping year, he succeeded in the eighteenth and reigned thirteen years. Inheriting the accumulated strength of the reigns of Guangwu and Ming, he turned the tone of government toward breadth and lenience; the *Hou Hanshu* records that "his rule was broad and lenient," and the historians called him "the elder-sage." The reigns of Ming and Zhang, with that of Guangwu, together form the golden age of early Eastern Han.

The cultural landmark of his reign was the White Tiger Hall Conference in the fourth Jianchu year: the scholars debated the agreements and differences of the Five Classics, the emperor pronounced the final verdict in person, and Ban Gu and others compiled the results as the *Baihu tongyi*, fixing the supremacy of the New-Text school. In administration he lightened punishments, encouraged farming, and relieved the people, summoning worthy men after earthquakes and other portents; in the Yuanhe years he made two progresses, south to Nanyang and north to Henei, performing the rite of ploughing the sacred field. On the frontier, carrying on the prestige of Ming's reign, Ban Chao broke Yanqi in the first Zhanghe year, and the states of the Western Regions returned to the Han map.

The lenient side had its price: in the third Jianchu year Dou was made empress, and the Dou clan grew in favor; by the time of Ma Fang's Qiang campaigns and Ban Chao's conquests, the consort families were already gathering strength. Zhang was not unaware of this in his later years, but he could never trim them. When he died in the second Zhanghe year, Emperor He was only ten; Empress Dowager Dou took the regency, and the Dou brothers dominated the government — consort-clan autocracy, from then on, became the chronic illness of Eastern Han politics.

Seen beside [[e-han-guangwu|Emperor Guangwu of Han]] and Emperor Ming, Zhang traces the path of Eastern Han from founding to consolidation: Guangwu settled the realm by the soft way, Ming disciplined it with strictness, and Zhang maintained it with lenience — achieving the Ming-Zhang age while handing the fuse of the consort-clan question to his successors.

> References: the punctuated critical edition of the *Hou Hanshu*, ch. 3 (Benji of Emperor Zhang), ch. 2 (Emperor Ming), and ch. 4 (Emperor He); the biographies of Ban Gu, Ban Chao, and the Annals of the Empresses; Tan Qixiang's atlas, vol. 2.
