# Emperor Xuan of Han · Principal Deeds

Liu Bingyi, an imperial great-grandson, was raised among the people after the witchcraft affair of Emperor Wu's last years and later entered the palace wards, ascending from the bottom and the margins of the court to the throne. This early life, "come from among the people, knowing the wickedness of the alleys and the gains and losses of officialdom," is the key to his later style of rule. This page takes the "Annals of Emperor Xuan" in the Book of Han as its chronological spine, supplemented by the biographies of Huo Guang, Zhao Chongguo, Zheng Ji, and the Xiongnu, and by the chronicle of the Zizhi Tongjian. The witchcraft affair was itself the aftershock of the late-reign court turmoil of [[han-wu-di|Emperor Wu of Han]], and the two reigns of great-grandfather and great-grandson can be read together.

His accession was itself the high-water mark of powerful-minister politics: in the first year of Yuanping, Emperor Zhao died, the King of Changyi was deposed, and Huo Guang and others installed the imperial great-grandson. At the start of the Benshi era the Huo clan still held power, and the emperor bided his time, husbanding his strength. In the second year of Dijie, Huo Guang died, and Xuan gradually drew back the reins; in the fourth year of Dijie, when Huo Yu's conspiracy failed and the Huo clan was exterminated, Xuan at last ruled in person. From enthronement to extermination, eight years completed the imperial house's reeling-in of the powerful minister.

His civil governance after assuming personal rule took "comprehensively checking names against reality" as its guiding principle: rewards were certain and punishments unfailing, officials were strictly examined, and reality was held accountable to names, forming a "consolidative" path of restoration distinct from Emperor Wu's expansion. Historical verdicts on the "Zhao-Xuan restoration" have never been uniform; this atlas takes the matching of names and reality as its primary label. Huo Guang's merits and faults, and Xuan's own political craft, are best read together.

War and frontier affairs form another thread: in the first year of Shenjue, Zhao Chongguo broke the Qiang through military agricultural colonies; in the second year, Zheng Ji combined the guardianship of the northern and southern routes under the title Protector General, and the Han court's institutionalized stationing in the Western Regions became the institutional resting point of Emperor Wu's expansion in Xuan's reign. During the Wufeng years the Xiongnu fell into civil strife, and Han frontier policy adjusted accordingly; in the third year of Ganlu, Chanyu Huhanye came to court as a vassal, opening a new phase in Han-Xiongnu relations and forming the frontier climax of the Zhao-Xuan era.

In the first year of Huanglong, Emperor Xuan died and Emperor Yuan succeeded; the administrative and frontier order accumulated under Zhao and Xuan passed into the next reign. Read alongside [[e-han-guangwu|Emperor Guangwu of Han]]: the Zhao-Xuan restoration and the Guangwu restoration are two different paths of rebuilding imperial order.
