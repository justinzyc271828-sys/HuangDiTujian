# Emperor Da of Wu · Principal Deeds

Sun Quan, styled Zhongmou, was Sun Jian's second son by Lady Wu. In the 5th year of Jian'an, Sun Ce died by an assassin's knife, and Quan, at nineteen, took command of Jiangdong with Zhang Zhao and Zhou Yu at his side — "raising the worthy and employing the able, each giving his utmost," and the Jiangdong foundations were secured. In the 13th year of Jian'an, as Cao Cao came south, Quan took the counsel of Zhou Yu and Lu Su, allied with Liu Bei, and broke the Cao army by fire at the Red Cliffs — the tripartite balance was fixed. In the 24th year Lü Meng's covert crossing seized Jingzhou, Guan Yu fell, and Wu held the Jing province. In the 1st year of Huangwu Lu Xun's fire attack at Yiling shattered Liu Bei, and Wu turned from defense to offense, its borders set.

In the 1st year of Huanglong Quan took the imperial throne at Wuchang, styling the realm Wu, and at once returned the capital to Jianye. Through the great ages of Cao Cao, Cao Pi, and Liu Bei, "employing talent and valuing stratagems, with the rare heroism of a Goujian," he held one leg of the tripod. In the decades of his empire he sent envoys to Gongsun Yuan in Liaodong and besieged the new city of Hefei, both fruitless, while governing Jiangdong gently — the people prospered, and for forty years there was no great war.

His late years were wrecked by the succession: in the 4th year of Chiwu the crown prince Deng died, and the two-palace feud followed; the court split between Prince Ba of Lu and the crown prince He, Quan deposed He, ordered Ba to die, and Lu Xun himself died of grief and rage — the "strife within the walls" began, and Wu's decline was already visible. In the 2nd year of Taiyuan he named his youngest son, Sun Liang, heir, and died, aged seventy-one, posthumously styled Da.

Chen Shou's verdict: "Sun Quan, bending himself and swallowing humiliation, employing talent and valuing stratagems, had the rare heroism of a Goujian — a man of eminence. So he could hold the south of the river and complete the tripod's reign. But his nature was jealous and suspicious, quick to kill, and in his last years it grew worse." "A son should be born like Sun Zhongmou" passed into the people's praise; yet the succession's double error was the distant cause of Wu's fall — a pity that a lifetime's hero could not keep his own house.

> References: the punctuated critical edition of the *Sanguozhi*, ch. 47 ("Biography of the Wu Sovereign"), ch. 54 (biography of Zhou Yu), ch. 58 (biography of Lu Xun).
