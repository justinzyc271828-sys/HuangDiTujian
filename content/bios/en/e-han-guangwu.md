# Emperor Guangwu of Han · Principal Deeds

Liu Xiu was the restoration sovereign who rebuilt the Han house amid the chaos at the end of the Xin dynasty. This page takes the 《后汉书·光武帝纪》 as its chronological backbone, cross-referenced with the biographies of the Yuntai generals and of Ma Yuan.

In Dihuang 3 of the Xin (22), he rose in arms at Chongling with imperial kinsmen and retainers, joining the tide of revolt against the Xin; the next year, at the Battle of Kunyang, he struck the many with the few and shattered the main Xin army of [[xin-wang-mang|Wang Mang]], establishing his military renown in a single stroke. During the Gengshi period he crossed the river to build a base in Hebei, gathering talent and drifting steadily away from the Gengshi regime; in Jianwu 1 (25) he took the imperial throne south of Hao, founded the Jianwu era, continued the Han dynastic name, and soon fixed the capital at Luoyang, thereby establishing the political center of the Eastern Han.

Once the state was founded, he wound down military ventures while shaping the political climate. In Jianwu 2 he broadly enfeoffed meritorious officials, and the Yuntai cohort took shape; in Jianwu 12 he defeated Gongsun Shu and other rivals, the realm was roughly pacified, and the restoration settled into place; in Jianwu 19 Ma Yuan and others pacified Jiaozhi, extending the martial record to the southern frontier. On the civil side, he prized the "way of softness," retired meritorious generals in favor of civil officials, and kept the generals from holding power; in Jianwu 15 he pushed through the land survey, auditing fields and households, which provoked local resistance and policy adjustments — the most contested node of his rule.

In his later years, in Zhongyuan 1 (56), he performed the Feng and Shan sacrifices at Mount Tai, the ritual high point of the restoration, which can be read against the Feng-Shan of [[han-wu-di|Emperor Wu of Han]]: one carried the unified empire's martial and ritual order to its zenith, the other used the same rites to announce to Heaven and Earth that success had been won from ruins. The following year (Zhongyuan 2, 57) he died at Luoyang; Emperor Ming succeeded him, and the Eastern Han dynastic line was preserved. Historians' verdicts focus on the "way of softness" and the land-survey controversy, and are on the whole favorable.
