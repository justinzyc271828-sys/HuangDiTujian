# Emperor Xuanzong of Tang · Principal Deeds

Li Longji, third son of Ruizong. In the 1st year of Tanglong he staged the Tanglong coup with the Princess Taiping and the others, slaying Empress Wei — the "re-founding of the Tang house" was his work; in the 1st year of Xiantian Ruizong yielded the throne; in the 1st year of Kaiyuan he had the Princess Taiping put to death, changed the era, and ruled in truth.

The Kaiyuan years he governed with vigor: Yao Chong, Song Jing, Zhang Yue, Zhang Jiuling — chancellor after chancellor — and the "Kaiyuan recovery" the histories call a golden age: "Remember the full days of Kaiyuan, when even the little towns held ten thousand households" (Du Fu). The institutions of Tang — the garrisons, the equal fields, the tax system — stood complete; the culture shone, and the ten thousand states came to court.

But in the Tianbao years he grew complacent: "trusting the long peace, he gave himself to luxury and slackened in government," doted on Consort Yang — "from this sovereign the court was no longer held at dawn" — made Li Linfu and Yang Guozhong chancellors, and nursed An Lushan at Fanyang. In the 14th year of Tianbao the An–Shi rebellion broke; An Lushan took Luoyang and styled himself emperor. In the 1st year of Zhide Tongguan fell; the emperor fled to Shu; at Mawei the soldiers mutinied and the consort was put to death — "though heaven and earth have their ends, this regret has none." His heir took the throne at Lingwu (Suzong), honoring him as Grand Emperor.

He died in the Shenlong Hall in the 1st year of Baoying, aged seventy-eight. The histories say: "Kaiyuan diligent, Tianbao complacent — one life, a whole age's rise and fall." In one man met the golden age and the beginning of the ruin: the splendor of Kaiyuan–Tianbao and the disaster of the An–Shi rebellion were both his — the classic mirror of a sovereign's rise and fall.

> References: the punctuated critical edition of the *Jiu Tangshu*, ch. 8 and 9 ("Benji of Emperor Xuanzong").
