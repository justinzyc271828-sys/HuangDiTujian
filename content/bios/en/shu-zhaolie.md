# Emperor Zhaolie of Han · Principal Deeds

Liu Bei, styled Xuande, was a native of Zhuoxian, Zhuojun, a descendant of Liu Sheng, Prince Jing of Zhongshan, son of Emperor Jing of Han. Orphaned young, he sold straw shoes and mats with his mother; yet he was ambitious, "delighting in company with heroes, so that the young flocked to him." In the 1st year of Zhongping the Yellow Turbans rose, and Bei led his followers into the army, earning by merit the commandancy of Anxi. Then began more than thirty years of drift through the realm — under Gongsun Zan, Tao Qian, Lü Bu, Cao Cao, Yuan Shao, Liu Biao — more defeats than victories, yet his "good faith was renowned within the four seas," and men of worth came to him.

In the 12th year of Jian'an, Zhuge Liang laid out the tripartite plan at Longzhong; in the 13th, at the Red Cliffs, the Sun–Liu allies broke Cao Cao, and Bei took the four commanderies of Jingzhou. In the 19th year he won Yizhou, and in the 24th, after the great struggle for Hanzhong, he routed the Cao army and took the title King of Hanzhong at Mianyang — the Hanzhong campaign fixed the tripartite balance. That same year Guan Yu lost Jingzhou and died at Jiangling, and the Shu–Wu alliance cracked.

In the 1st year of Zhangwu, on the rumored death of Emperor Xian, Bei took the throne at Chengdu, styling his realm Han in continuation of the Han line. To avenge Guan Yu he led the host against Wu in person that July, deaf to Zhao Yun and Zhuge Liang. In the 2nd year of Zhangwu, Lu Xun burned the linked camps at Yiling; Bei was shattered, escaped with his life alone, and fell back to White Emperor City. He died in the Yong'an Palace the next year at sixty-three, entrusting his heir to Zhuge Liang: "If the heir can be aided, aid him; if he is unworthy, take the throne yourself."

Chen Shou's verdict: "magnanimous, resolute, and generous, discerning in men and generous to officers — the manner of Gaozu and the bearing of a hero," yet "his cunning and resource fell short of the martial Wei." A shoemaker who never despaired through defeat after defeat, he yet built a corner of the tripartite realm; his last charge — "do no evil because it is small, omit no good because it is small" — and his name for benevolence outlived him, making him the most beloved of the Three Kingdoms' sovereigns.

> References: the punctuated critical edition of the *Sanguozhi*, ch. 32 ("Biography of the Former Sovereign"), ch. 35 (biography of Zhuge Liang), ch. 47 ("Biography of the Wu Sovereign").
