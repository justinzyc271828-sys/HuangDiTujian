# Emperor Jing of Wu · Principal Deeds

Sun Xiu, styled Zilie, was Sun Quan's sixth son, enfeoffed Prince of Langya, dwelling at Hulin. In the 3rd year of Taiping, Sun Chen deposed Sun Liang and brought in Xiu, changing the era to Yong'an. Xiu knew Chen's treachery: on accession he honored Chen with gifts to put him at ease, while secretly plotting with the old ministers Zhang Bu and Ding Feng. In the twelfth month of the 1st year of Yong'an, at the winter festival court, the trap closed and Sun Chen was slain before the hall, his kin exterminated — for the first time since Zhuge Ke, Sun Jun, and Sun Chen, Wu's government returned to the sovereign.

In person rule Xiu governed diligently: he decreed chairs of the Five Classics, established academies, and fostered learning; he lightened taxes, relieved the stricken, and examined the officials — much that was admirable. But he "delighted in law and punishment," and used them too far: Zhang Bu, presuming on his merit, fell under suspicion and was slain — the cunning that slew a usurper and the harshness that slew a supporter met in one man. Chen Shou judged him "not without fault, yet able to repair his faults": in the middle of Wu's decline, a restorer in the making.

In the 6th year of Yong'an, with Shu fallen and the Wei armies massed on the border, Xiu fell ill; the crown prince was a child, and he entrusted the heir to the chancellor Puru Yangxing. He died that July at thirty, posthumously styled Jing. After his death Yangxing and the others, deeming the child heir too weak for the hour, set aside the late sovereign's will and brought in Sun Hao, marquis of Wucheng — the precedent of enthronement by coup that undid Wu. Six years of slaying Sun Chen, founding schools, and ruling in person were the good deeds of a competent sovereign; his early death was Wu's misfortune.

> References: the punctuated critical edition of the *Sanguozhi*, ch. 48 ("Biographies of the Three Successors"), ch. 64 (biography of Sun Chen).
