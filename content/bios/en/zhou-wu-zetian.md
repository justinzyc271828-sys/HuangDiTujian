# Wu Zetian · Key Events

Wu Zetian is the only woman in Chinese history to have formally taken the imperial title. This page builds its chronology on the "Basic Annals of Empress Zetian" in the Old Book of Tang, supplemented by the New Book of Tang and the Tang chronicle of the Zizhi Tongjian. She entered the palace in Zhenguan 11 as a cairen consort of [[tang-tai-zong|Emperor Taizong of Tang]] and, after the Ganye Temple episode, returned to the court; in Yonghui 6 she was raised from zhaoyi to empress. In the struggle over deposing Empress Wang for Wu, veteran opponents such as Zhangsun Wuji were demoted and died one after another, and through this she entered the center of power.

After Xianqing 5, as Gaozong fell frequently ill, the Heavenly Empress joined in deciding state affairs and a "Two Sages" arrangement took shape at court. In Hongdao 1 Gaozong died in Luoyang; Zhongzong succeeded and she ruled from behind the screen. The very next year she deposed Zhongzong to Prince of Luling, installed Ruizong instead, and held real power over the court. In Chuigong 4 the Bright Hall in Luoyang was completed, while copper-box informant reports and the politics of harsh officials ran in tandem: her dual-track statecraft of favor and fear took form here.

In Tianshou 1, at the Divine Capital Luoyang, she took a new honorific title, changed the dynastic name to Zhou, and formally assumed the throne; rule by a female sovereign passed from fact into name. During her reign she broke conventions in appointing talent and developed the palace examination within the imperial examinations, and in Shengong 1 Di Renjie's entry as chancellor became the representative node of capable-official politics. Solid civil achievements and the rule of harsh officials coexisted throughout, forming the two faces of Wu Zhou.

In Shenlong 1, Zhang Jianzhi and others launched a coup that restored Zhongzong; Wu Zhou ended and Zetian moved to another palace. The old histories mostly belittled her through the "female calamity" narrative, while today her institutional and personnel legacy is being revalued. This atlas's appraisal is neutral to mildly negative, left for readers to weigh against the evidence: from palace cairen to changing Tang into Zhou and then returning power to the Li Tang house, read alongside the restoration of [[tang-xian-zong|Emperor Xianzong of Tang]], one can see the full arc of the Tang house's mid-course decline and rebuilding.
