# Emperor Zhao of Han · Principal Deeds

Liu Fuling was the youngest son of [[han-wu-di|Emperor Wu of Han]], by the Hook-Yi Lady. In the second year of the Later era, with Emperor Wu ill, the eight-year-old Fuling was made heir apparent, and General-in-Chief Huo Guang received the testamentary charge as regent; when Wu died, the heir took the throne. An eight-year-old emperor faced the realm drained by the late campaigns of Wu's reign, a jealous royal house, and watchful regents — and yet the Zhao reign passed thirteen years in stability under Huo Guang's hand. The historians said he "knew the essentials of the times."

The policy line of his reign was rest and recuperation: in the second month of the sixth Shiyuan year he ordered the commanderies to recommend worthy and literary men and asked them about the people's grievances, opening the debate over the salt, iron, and liquor monopolies and the equal-transport system — the Salt and Iron Debate — after which the liquor monopoly was abolished. Corvée and taxes were lightened and the people rested, in the spirit of the Wen-Jing age. On the frontier, in the fourth Yuanfeng year, Fu Jiezi went to the Western Regions, slew the Loulan king Angui by stratagem, and renamed the state Shanshan, preserving the prestige of Han in that direction.

The court was not quiet. In the Shiyuan years the king of Yan plotted with Shangguan Jie, Sang Hongyang, and others to kill Huo Guang and depose the emperor; Zhao himself detected the forgery of the plot's letter. The affair failed, the plotters were executed with their clans, and Huo Guang came to dominate the court absolutely. The histories credit Zhao with early wisdom and a clear eye in judging cases, yet after the age of fourteen real power belonged entirely to Huo Guang; in his later years the emperor's figure recedes into the background of the regency.

In the fourth month of the first Yuanping year Emperor Zhao died at twenty-one, without issue. Huo Guang first summoned [[han-changyi|Prince He of Changyi]]; He was deposed after twenty-seven dissolute days, and Emperor Wu's great-grandson [[han-xuan-di|Emperor Xuan of Han]] was raised to the throne. The recuperative policies and regency structure accumulated in the Zhao reign continued as the Zhao-Xuan recovery, the last tranquil age of the Western Han's middle period.
