# The Deposed Emperor of Western Wei · Principal Deeds

Yuan Qin, eldest son of Emperor Wen, took the throne on his father's death in the 17th year of Datong — the histories note he "fixed no era name," a move of Yuwen Tai's to strip away the sinicizing forms and restore Xianbei ways. Three years he reigned, doing nothing, the government wholly Tai's: in the 3rd year the Western Wei armies broke Jiangling and destroyed Emperor Yuan of Liang, "taking the treasures of his storehouses and the books of Song, Qi, and Liang" — the cultural wealth of the south passed into Guanzhong.

The Deposed Emperor would not be a puppet: "the emperor plotted secretly to slay Tai, and Tai learned of it." In the 3rd year Tai deposed Qin as Duke of Anding, set up his brother, the Prince of Qi, Kuo, and soon poisoned Qin, aged thirty. The histories say: "he would have stood on his own, and failed." Since Xiaowu's flight west, the power of the Western Wei throne was wholly the Yuwens'; Qin's attempt to restore it ended in deposition and death.

His fall fixed the "empty throne" of the Guanzhong bloc: from the Gong emperor of Western Wei through the emperors of Northern Zhou, the throne was the Yuwens' instrument. And the Jiangling conquest brought the south's books and institutions into Guanzhong — the sources of Sui–Tang institutions, gathered under Western Wei.
