# Emperor Wu of Qi · Principal Deeds

Xiao Ze, styled Xuanyuan, was the eldest son of Emperor Gao. On his father's death in the 4th year of Jianyuan he took the throne at forty-two, changing the era to Yongming. Eleven years he carried on Gao's frugality with a lenient hand: he encouraged farming and mulberries, lightened the levies and the corvée, simplified the punishments, honored the Confucian learning and built the schools — the people prospered, and the "Yongming recovery" became, after Yuanjia, the second era of modest plenty in the southern dynasties.

The Yongming years shone in culture: the emperor and the Prince of Jingling, Xiao Ziliang, opened the western mansion, gathering Shen Yue, Xie Tiao, Wang Rong, and the rest, who worked out the rules of tone — the "Yongming style," fountainhead of the regulated verse. The Eight Friends of the Jingling were the glory of the southern courts. But after the 5th year of Yongming, Qi and Wei held the border without decision, and the north gained nothing; in his last years the Crown Prince Wenhui died early, and his grandson Zhaoye was made heir — "and the young sovereign's misrule followed."

He died in the 11th year of Yongming at fifty-four; his grandson Zhaoye succeeded. The histories say he "attended to government and kept it simple," yet "trusted the petty men, and the court's discipline slackened." The Yongming recovery was the last glow of the southern golden age: within ten years of his death Xiao Luan usurped, the Marquis of Donghun raged, Xiao Yan took the realm — the glory of Yongming passed like a dream.

> References: the punctuated critical edition of the *Nanqishu*, ch. 3 ("Benji of Emperor Wu"), ch. 52 (biographies of the literary men).
