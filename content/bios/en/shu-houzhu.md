# The Later Sovereign of Shu · Principal Deeds

Liu Shan, styled Gongsi, was the Former Sovereign's son by Lady Gan. Born in Jingzhou in the 12th year of Jian'an, he was saved at the Changban disaster by Zhao Yun's single-handed ride. On the Former Sovereign's death in the 3rd year of Zhangwu, Shan took the throne at Chengdu at seventeen, changing the era to Jianxing, with Zhuge Liang as regent by the late will; nothing in government was decided without Liang. In the 3rd year of Jianxing Liang pacified the south, capturing Meng Huo seven times; in the 5th he memorialized the *Chu Shi Biao*, garrisoned Hanzhong, and campaigned north against Wei year after year. Liang's rule — "laws and education strict and clear, rewards and punishments sure" — made Shu's government the finest of its age: the histories say that with worthy ministers the Later Sovereign ruled aright.

In the 12th year of Jianxing Liang died at the Wuzhang Plain; Jiang Wan and Fei Yi governed in turn, keepers of the established order, and Shu rested. In the 16th year of Yanxi Fei Yi was assassinated, and Jiang Wei took the armies, campaigning into Longxi year after year with little gain while the state's strength ebbed; the sovereign took the eunuch Huang Hao into his confidence, government decayed daily, and Jiang Wei, to escape the eunuch's malice, went to farm at Tazhong.

In the 6th year of Jingyao Wei invaded on three roads; Deng Ai came by the Yinping track over impossible ground, broke Zhuge Zhan at Mianzhu, and reached the gates of Chengdu. On Qiao Zhou's counsel the sovereign surrendered with the coffin in his cart — Shu Han, through two sovereigns and forty-three years, was no more. Brought with his house to Luoyang, he was made Duke of Anle by Wei. At a banquet Sima Zhao asked whether he thought of Shu; he answered, "I am happy here and do not think of Shu" — "happier here than in Shu" passing into the language as a byword.

The historians' judgment: with worthy ministers he was a right-reasoning sovereign, beguiled by eunuchs a darkened one — the state's fate turned on the men he chose. That Shu held forty years was the work of Zhuge Liang, Jiang Wan, and Fei Yi; that it fell was the work of Huang Hao's power, Jiang Wei's exhausting wars, and the failure of relief. Yet "happier here" kept him alive in an age of slaughter — the self-preservation of a weakling, and perhaps a kind of wisdom.
