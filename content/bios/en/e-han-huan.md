# Emperor Huan of Han · Principal Deeds

Liu Zhi was the son of the marquis Yi of Liwu. After [[e-han-zhi|Emperor Zhi]] was poisoned, Liang Ji raised the fifteen-year-old Zhi to the throne as Emperor Huan. For the next fourteen years the government lay entirely in Liang Ji's hands: his house held seven marquises, three empresses, and six titled ladies, and ruled for twenty years while the ministers looked on askance.

Huan bided his time for fourteen years, and in the second Yanxi year seized his chance: using the eunuchs Shan Chao, Xu Huang, Ju Yuan, Zuo Guan, and Tang Heng, he sent troops to take Liang Ji's seal of office; Liang Ji and his wife Sun Shou killed themselves, and the entire Liang house, kin included, was destroyed. But the cure was worse than the disease: the eunuchs replaced the consorts as the dominant power — Shan Chao and the other four were enfeoffed the same day, the "Five Marquises," and the eunuch scourge burned brighter than ever. In the fourth Yanxi year salaries from the Three Excellencies down were cut for lack of funds — a measure of the realm's straits.

The deepest-reaching event of his reign was the proscription of the partisans: in the ninth Yanxi year the celebrity officials headed by Li Ying, colonel of the capital, clashed head-on with the eunuch faction; Li Ying and others were imprisoned and the first proscription began; in the first Yongkang year the partisans were pardoned and sent home, but barred from office for life. The duel between the scholar-officials and the eunuchs became the axis of late Eastern Han politics from this point on. In the twelfth month of Yongkang 1, Huan died at thirty-six, without issue; Dou Wu raised the marquis of Xieduting, [[e-han-ling|Liu Hong]], as Emperor Ling. The historians wrote that Huan's earlier records are fragmentary and "dirtied with dust" — he destroyed Liang Ji only to unleash the Five Marquises, sounding the prelude to the eunuch autocracy of Ling's reign.

> References: the punctuated critical edition of the *Hou Hanshu*, ch. 7 (Benji of Emperor Huan), and the biographies of Liang Ji, the Eunuchs, and the Proscribed Partisans; Tan Qixiang's atlas, vol. 2.
