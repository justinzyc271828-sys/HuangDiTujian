# Emperor Zhi of Han · Principal Deeds

Liu Zuan was the son of the filial king Hong of Bohai. In the first Yongjia year, after [[e-han-chong|Emperor Chong]] died, Empress Dowager Liang and Liang Ji chose the eight-year-old Zuan as Emperor Zhi. That was the beginning of his tragedy, and the summit of Liang autocracy in Eastern Han.

Zhi was sharp-witted as a child; the histories say he was "quick and clever from youth." In the first Benchu year, looking straight at Liang Ji in the court, he said the words that changed his life — "This is the arrogant general." Liang Ji hated him for it, and in the intercalary sixth month of the same year had poisoned cakes brought to the emperor. The poisoned boy begged for water to save himself; Liang Ji refused, and the emperor died, having reigned a little over a year. Historians have pitied the sharp boy and mourned his early end: an eight-year-old, killed for one true sentence, the first victim of the duel between Han sovereignty and its overbearing minister.

After Zhi's death Liang Ji raised the marquis of Liwu, [[e-han-huan|Liu Zhi]], as Emperor Huan, and the Liang scourge ran another thirteen years until Huan destroyed it with the eunuchs in the second Yanxi year. Zhi's death is the footnote to Liang Ji's power, and the origin of the士人's collective memory of the word "arrogant" — "the arrogant general" has been the eternal epithet of the overbearing minister ever since.

> References: the punctuated critical edition of the *Hou Hanshu*, ch. 6 (Benji of Emperor Zhi) and the biography of Liang Ji; Tan Qixiang's atlas, vol. 2.
