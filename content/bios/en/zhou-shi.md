# Emperor Shizong of Later Zhou · Key Events

Chai Rong reigned barely six years, yet he was the hinge between what came before and after at the close of the Five Dynasties: he inherited the newly founded enterprise of his adoptive father Guo Wei, Taizu of Later Zhou, and opened the way to the unification completed under [[n-song-tai-zu|Emperor Taizu of Song]] Zhao Kuangyin. This page builds its chronology on the "Annals of Shizong" in the Zhou shu of the Old History of the Five Dynasties, supplemented by the "Zhou Basic Annals" in the New History of the Five Dynasties and the Zizhi Tongjian.

In Xiande 1 Guo Wei died and Chai Rong succeeded, keeping the Xiande era name. When the allied armies of Northern Han and the Khitans pressed the border, he campaigned in person and defeated them at Gaoping; after the battle he purged and reorganized the palace army, establishing a new ruler's authority in a single stroke. His domestic policy was equally decisive: in Xiande 2 he restricted temples, reduced the clergy, and melted bronze Buddha statues for coin to fund finances and armament; across the Xiande years he further selected and trained the palace army and equalized land taxes, bringing state power back up.

In the south he campaigned against Southern Tang year after year, raising armies in Xiande 3 and maneuvering repeatedly in Huainan through Xiande 4 and 5, until in Xiande 5 Southern Tang ceded the lands north of the river and Zhou took fourteen prefectures, pushing the frontier to the north bank of the Yangzi, the very precursor of the passive position later faced by the line of [[n-tang-houzhu|Li Yu, last ruler of Southern Tang]]. In Xiande 6 he turned to a northern expedition against the Khitans, recovered the Three Passes and prefectures of Guannan with his spearhead pointed toward Youzhou, but fell gravely ill in the field and had to turn back.

Back in Kaifeng he entrusted his young heir to Fan Zhi and others and died soon after; his son Zongxun succeeded as a child. The next year came the Chenqiao Mutiny, and Zhou yielded to Song; his unfinished ambition of unification was taken up and completed by the Song. Historians often dwell on his early death: had he been granted long years, the opening chapter of Song history might have been rewritten. That counterfactual is the most thought-provoking knot in reading the history of the late Five Dynasties.
